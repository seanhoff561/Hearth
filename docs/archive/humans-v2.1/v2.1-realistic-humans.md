# AMENDMENT V2.1 — Realistic Humans
## Replacing the agent framework with a genetics-to-society human simulation

You are the autonomous engine programmer building `hearth`. You're currently working on **V2-11 (Australopithecus & the agent framework)** from the v2 direction-change prompt (`hearth_v2_direction_change_prompt.md`, called **v2** below). This document is **V2.1**. It changes the design of that framework before it hardens. Read all of it before changing code.

**The goal in one sentence:** the world is populated by simulated people who are born as babies to two parents, inherit realistic genetic variation, grow up inside a family and a culture, learn what the people around them know, form relationships, work, argue, cooperate, raise children, grow old and die. Running many of them together, over generations, should realistically reproduce how human societies develop. When the player picks an era, the people they meet should feel like real people *of that era*: what they know, how they live, what they believe, and how they treat a stranger.

### 0.3 Realism vs feasibility: the substitutions
Not all of this can be simulated literally. Where it can't, use the substitute below and say so in `docs/design/humans/feasibility.md`. **Don't quietly drop the goal; replace it with the best feasible model of it.**

| Ideal | Why it's infeasible | Substitute |
|---|---|---|
| A real human genome (3 billion base pairs) | Memory, compute and no real meaning in a game | An abstract diploid genome of a few hundred loci on 23 chromosome pairs, with recombination, calibrated so traits show realistic heritability (§4) |
| A real human mind | Unsolved science and compute | A layered cognitive architecture: reflexes → routines → utility selection → hierarchical planning → social reasoning, driven by personality, emotion, memory and belief (§6) |
| Every human on the planet simulated individually across hundreds of thousands of years | Billions of agent-years | Three simulation tiers (§17): a deep-time abstract model of populations, cultures, languages and knowledge; a household layer for the recent past and the wider region; full individuals near the player |
| Natural language conversation | No offline NLU at this quality; nondeterministic | A structured **speech-act** system with generated languages and text rendering, plus an **optional, pluggable** language-model dialogue layer that can never change simulation state outside validated speech acts (§10) |
| Real historical peoples and religions | Doesn't fit a random planet; invites stereotypes | **Generated fictional cultures, languages and belief systems** whose *structure* is realistic for their era, subsistence and environment (§9) |
| Infinite behavioral variety | Authoring cost | Generative behavior from personality × culture × situation × memory, plus routines and norms authored as data |

### 0.4 Definition of "realistic human" (the acceptance standard)
An observer who knows ethnography and history, watching a group for a game week, should find that:
1. **Individuals differ** in temperament, ability, appearance and choices in ways that partly run in families, without being caricatures.
2. **Everyone has a history:** parents, siblings, a birthplace, past events they remember, relationships with grudges and affections.
3. **People only know what they've learned.** They make mistakes, hold false beliefs, explain the world through their culture's ideas, and can be surprised, deceived or persuaded.
4. **Behavior fits the era and culture:** daily routine, work, food, dress, tools, group size, attitudes to strangers, child-rearing, ritual and social order are plausible for that way of life.
5. **Groups change over generations** for intelligible reasons: population, environment, contact, innovation, conflict.
6. **Nothing looks scripted.** Repeated observation shows variation, not loops.

## 1. Ground rules for sensitive content (non-negotiable)

Realism here touches genetics, children, reproduction, violence and culture. These rules are both scientifically accurate and necessary.

1. **Genetics and ancestry.** Genes create **individual** variation in temperament, aptitudes and health within every population. The model must contain **no behavioral, cognitive or moral trait whose distribution differs by ancestry, population or appearance.** Physical traits can differ between populations where real selection pressures explain it (skin pigmentation with UV, lactase persistence with dairying, altitude adaptation), and those differences must have no behavioral consequences. Differences in behavior between groups come only from culture, environment, history and circumstances. Add a lint/test that fails if any behavioral trait's genetic baseline is parameterized by population.
2. **Children are never targets.** The player can't harm children, and no game system lets the player or anyone else target children. Childhood illness and death exist demographically, as they did historically, but they happen off-screen and are communicated with respect (a family in mourning, a small grave), never shown graphically.
3. **Reproduction is abstracted.** Courtship and pair bonds are social relationships. Conception happens off-screen. Pregnancy and birth are life events, not shown explicitly. There's no sexual content of any kind. Pair bonds form only by mutual relationship (both agents' preferences and the relationship state), with no coercion mechanics, including for the player.
4. **Violence and historical institutions.** Conflict, raiding and warfare exist as real historical dynamics, simulated and shown at a non-gratuitous level. There are no torture or cruelty mechanics. Hierarchy, inequality, servitude and similar institutions in later eras are represented as **social structures in the simulation** (status, obligations, who can leave), described soberly and never as a player reward loop.
5. **Fictional cultures only.** Generated peoples, languages and belief systems never imitate or caricature real ethnic groups or religions. Their structures (kinship systems, subsistence, ritual forms, political organization) follow cross-cultural patterns known from anthropology.
6. **Gender.** Era-typical divisions of labor and social roles emerge from culture data, which varies between cultures (no single universal script), and individuals vary within any culture. Culture constrains agents; it never restricts what the player can do.

Record these in `docs/design/humans/ground-rules.md` and enforce what can be enforced in code and lint.

## 4. Genetics

### 4.1 Genome representation
- **23 chromosome pairs** (22 autosomes + XX/XY), each with a genetic length in centimorgans roughly proportional to real chromosomes.
- On them, **~300–600 abstract loci** grouped by function: pigmentation (skin, hair, eye), hair form, facial and body morphology, height and build, sex-limited traits, disease susceptibility, metabolism (including lactase persistence, altitude and cold adaptation, starch digestion), immunity diversity (a simplified HLA-like region whose diversity affects disease resistance), and **temperament and aptitude** loci (many small-effect loci per trait, i.e. polygenic). Store alleles as small integers; a genome is a few hundred bytes.
- Per-locus allele frequencies are kept per **gene pool** (a population in the deep-time layer, §15.1), so a person's ancestry statistically shapes physical traits. Behavioral loci have the **same** frequencies in every pool (§1.1).

### 4.2 Inheritance
- **Meiosis:** for each parent and each chromosome, simulate crossovers (count from the genetic length, positions random), producing a recombinant gamete. Child = one gamete from each parent. Sex from the father's X or Y.
- **Mutation:** a small per-locus rate creating new allele values (mostly neutral or mildly harmful), so variation is replenished and drift has something to work on.
- **Inbreeding:** homozygosity at recessive deleterious loci increases health risks in children of close relatives. Realistic, and it gives cultures a reason to develop exogamy norms (§9).
- **Lineage:** each person stores parent ids. Genealogies are queryable (kin coefficient between any two persons in the registry), used for kin altruism (§8) and inbreeding.

### 4.3 Genotype → phenotype
Use additive polygenic models plus a few dominance and interaction effects where real biology has them. **Calibrate** each trait so heritability across the simulated population lands in a realistic range (record targets and their sources in data; mark uncertain ones):
- **Physical appearance:** skin pigmentation (polygenic, high heritability), hair color and form, eye color (a few major-effect loci plus modifiers, giving realistic family patterns such as two brown-eyed parents having a blue-eyed child), facial features and proportions within the blocky model's parameters, height (high heritability, but strongly affected by childhood nutrition and illness, §4.4), build. The phenotype drives the **character model** directly, so families *look* related.
- **Health:** disease susceptibilities, resistance diversity, recessive conditions (abstracted, non-graphic), metabolic adaptations, longevity tendencies, fertility variation.
- **Temperament** (the root of personality, §5): baseline levels on a validated personality model (use the **HEXACO** six factors or Big Five; pick one and justify in `DECISIONS.md`), plus narrower dimensions useful for behavior: behavioral inhibition (shy vs bold), stress reactivity, impulsivity vs self-control, novelty seeking, sociability, aggressiveness threshold, empathy. Target **moderate heritability (roughly 0.3–0.5)**, with the rest from environment and chance, as twin studies find.
- **Aptitudes:** modest individual variation in learning rates by domain (motor skill, spatial, social, verbal, memory) and stamina. Effects are deliberately **small relative to practice and teaching**: in the game, as in life, skill comes mostly from experience.
- **Sex differences:** average differences in body size and strength following real dimorphism. Avoid hard-coding behavioral sex differences; any average behavioral differences emerge from culture and roles (§1.6).

### 4.4 Development: genes × environment
Phenotypes **unfold** over the life course; they're not fixed at birth:
- Childhood nutrition and illness affect adult height, health and immunity (malnourished children grow up shorter, as historical skeletons show).
- Early-life stress, attachment quality (§7.3) and family environment shift temperament expression within its genetic range (e.g., stress reactivity), and how much depends on the person's genes (gene × environment interaction for a few traits, kept simple).
- Practice changes skill; aging changes strength, speed, memory and health.
- Expose the full chain in the dev inspector: genotype score → developmental adjustments → current phenotype.

### 4.5 Population genetics (deep time)
At the abstract tier (§15.1), gene pools evolve through drift (strong in small, isolated groups), founder effects when groups split off and colonize, gene flow between neighbors, and slow **natural selection** on physical and health loci only: pigmentation toward UV-appropriate levels over long times, lactase persistence where dairying cultures persist, altitude adaptation in long-settled highlands, disease resistance after epidemics. Archaic admixture between hominin species where they meet is handled at the gene-pool level only. Over the short spans of play, selection is negligible, as it should be.

### 4.6 The player's genome
- The character creator (v2 §9.1) **solves for a genotype** consistent with the chosen appearance (search over pigmentation/morphology loci), and draws temperament and aptitude loci at random from the human distribution. The player's temperament has no mechanical effect on the player (the person at the keyboard is the mind), but it's inherited by their children.
- Children the player has with a partner inherit from both genomes and look like a believable mix of the two.

## 5. Psyche: personality, emotion, values

### 5.1 Personality
- Adult personality = temperament (genetic baseline, §4.3) shaped by development (§4.4) and culture (§9): each culture shifts how traits are *expressed* (e.g., display rules for anger), not the traits themselves.
- Personality maps to **behavior parameters** through data curves: risk tolerance, curiosity/exploration rate (which drives innovation, §11.4), patience and time preference, cooperativeness, trust in strangers, conformity bias (how strongly they copy the majority), prestige bias (copying successful people), emotional reactivity, aggression thresholds, forgiveness rate, conscientiousness at work, sociability (time spent with others), dominance-seeking.
- **Effect sizes must be modest.** Personality tilts choices; situation, culture and relationships usually dominate. Tests should show statistically significant but realistic correlations between traits and behaviors (not "the aggressive one always fights").

### 5.2 Emotion and mood
- **Appraisal-based emotions:** events are appraised against the person's goals, values and relationships (threat to self or kin → fear; blocked goal or insult → anger; loss → grief; norm violation by self → shame or guilt; by others → indignation; success → joy and pride; contamination → disgust; affiliation → affection). Intensity depends on personality and context; emotions decay over time.
- Emotions **change behavior** (fear narrows choices toward safety; anger raises confrontation; grief withdraws), **show on the body** (posture, animation, face on the blocky model, voice), and **are visible to other agents**, who react (comforting, avoiding, joining).
- **Mood** integrates emotions and body state (hunger, pain, fatigue, cold) over days; **stress** accumulates from chronic threats and losses and affects health, sleep and decisions.
- **Grief and attachment:** deaths of close kin cause lasting grief and mourning behavior shaped by culture.

### 5.3 Values
Internalized cultural values (§9.2) weight what a person cares about: kin loyalty, generosity, honor, autonomy, deference to elders, piety, fairness, courage. Values vary within a culture by personality and life experience. Acting against one's values causes guilt; seeing others do so causes judgment.

## 6. Mind: cognitive architecture

### 6.1 Layers (cheap to expensive)
1. **Reflexes** (every tick, near-free): flinch, flee from sudden threat, catch balance, look toward a sudden noise.
2. **Routines** (scripted but parameterized, data): culturally specific daily and seasonal routines (wake, tend fire, eat, work tasks by role and season, socialize at evening fire, sleep), which make ordinary life look ordinary at low cost.
3. **Utility selection** (staggered, roughly every few game seconds per agent): scores candidate goals from needs (v2 body + social and psychological needs: belonging, status, autonomy, safety of kin, meaning through ritual), emotions, obligations, personality and opportunities; switches away from routine when something matters more.
4. **Hierarchical planning** (on goal change, budgeted): an HTN planner turns goals into actions using the process engine, construction system and movement (e.g., "make a spear" → find straight shaft, get flint, knap point, haft with sinew and pitch), and replans when things fail.
5. **Social reasoning** (event-driven): who to ask, who to trust, what others probably want or know (lightweight theory of mind: per-relationship beliefs about intentions and knowledge), whether to share, help, lie, confront, apologize or avoid.
Species profiles cap planning depth and social reasoning (Australopithecus plans shallowly; *H. sapiens* plans multi-step and seasonally).

### 6.2 Perception
Reuse the v2 animal sensing model (vision, hearing, smell where relevant) with human parameters, plus **social perception**: recognizing individuals, reading emotions from posture and voice, noticing who is with whom, noticing norm violations. Agents act only on what they perceive or remember. No omniscience.

### 6.3 Memory and belief
- **Episodic memory:** salient events (who, what, where, when, emotional weight) with decay by salience and rehearsal; important ones are retold as stories (§11.2) and become shared memory.
- **Semantic memory:** knowledge of places (resource patches, water, dangers, territories, as a personal mental map that can be wrong or out of date), people (names, kin, reputations), and the world (the knowledge graph, §11).
- **Beliefs** can be false: agents can be deceived, misremember, act on rumors, and hold culturally given explanations (illness from spirits or broken taboos; seasons as gifts of ancestors). Beliefs update from evidence and testimony weighted by trust and conformity.
- Memory is **compressed into LifeHistory** over time so long-lived agents stay bounded in size (keep the defining memories: births, deaths, marriages, fights, migrations, discoveries, meeting the player).

### 6.4 Budgets
Each layer has a per-tick budget and level-of-detail rules (§17). An agent out of the player's sight can plan less often and in coarser steps; an agent talking to the player gets full attention.

## 7. Life course

All of this runs on the calendar time scale (§3), with ranges set per species and per era from data.

### 7.1 Pair bonding, pregnancy and birth
- Pair bonds form from relationships (§8.2) shaped by culture's marriage system (§9.2), personal attraction (a mix of compatibility, status and familiarity; no explicit content) and family influence where the culture gives families a say.
- Conception is a probability per cycle for bonded pairs, affected by age, nutrition, breastfeeding (which spaces births realistically), health and fertility genes. It's off-screen.
- Pregnancy is visible and has realistic effects on the mother's body (v2 physiology: energy needs, mobility late in pregnancy). Birth is an event with real (era-dependent) risks, attended by kin. Twins happen at realistic rates.

### 7.2 Life stages
| Stage | Realistic behavior |
|---|---|
| **Infant** (0–2) | Carried, nursed, kept close; crawling and first steps; attachment forms; strangers cause wariness from ~8 months |
| **Young child** (2–6) | Stays near caregivers; imitates adults; play (pretend play with tool-like objects, chasing, building); starts language; small helpful chores where culture expects them |
| **Child** (6–12) | Mixed-age play groups; learns skills by watching and joining in (foraging, gathering firewood, minding younger children, tending animals in herding cultures); learns norms, stories and taboos |
| **Adolescent** (12–18) | Gradually takes adult tasks; apprenticeship to skilled kin; rites of passage where culture has them; peer status and courtship begin late in this stage |
| **Adult** (18–45) | Full work roles, pair bonds, raising children, decision-making, peak physical ability |
| **Mature adult** (45–65) | Experience and knowledge peak; leadership, teaching, grandparenting (a major real contributor to child survival) |
| **Elder** (65+) | Declining strength; knowledge keepers and storytellers; cared for or (in hard times and some cultures) a burden; death from age-related illness |

Ages are approximate defaults per species and culture in data. Children use **child body models** with realistic proportions, smaller sizes, higher voices and their own animation sets.

### 7.3 Development
- **Attachment:** responsive caregiving produces secure attachment (confidence, exploration); neglect or loss produces insecurity (§4.4 effects). Alloparenting (grandparents, siblings, aunts and uncles) is normal and matters.
- **Language acquisition:** children acquire their culture's language(s) by exposure, fastest when young; bilingual children in contact zones (§10).
- **Skill and norm learning** by imitation, play, practice and teaching (§11). What a child learns depends on who they spend time with.
- **Nutrition and health** in childhood shape adult phenotype (§4.4).

### 7.4 Aging, death and mourning
- Aging degrades strength, speed, senses, fertility (menopause), and raises illness risk; lifespan varies with genes, health history, environment and era.
- **Death** from injury, illness, childbirth, starvation, predators, conflict or old age, at rates that match era life tables (§14.3). Bodies are treated according to culture (burial, cremation, exposure, grave goods). Mourning, grief, inheritance of possessions and roles, and remembrance (ancestors in stories) follow.

## 8. Social life

### 8.1 Kinship and households
- A full **kinship graph** (parents, siblings, children, spouses, in-laws, cousins), interpreted through each culture's **kinship system** (data: which relatives are grouped and named, descent rules, which kin you can marry, who inherits).
- **Households** (the basic economic unit: who shares a hearth and food) and larger groupings by era: bands, clans and lineages, villages, towns.

### 8.2 Relationships
For each pair that knows each other: familiarity, affection, trust, respect, attraction (adult peers only), fear, rivalry, and a ledger of **obligations** (favors given and owed, gifts, debts, insults unanswered). Relationships change through interactions and shared experience, and decay without contact. Each person tracks a realistic number of relationships: an inner circle of ~5, close ties of ~15, a social world of up to ~150 known individuals, and a wider set of recognizable acquaintances.

### 8.3 Cooperation and exchange
Food sharing (central in foragers, with sharing norms for big game and household autonomy for gathered food), helping, kin altruism scaled by relatedness, reciprocity with memory of fairness, gift exchange to build alliances, collective tasks (communal hunts, building, harvests), and care for the sick and injured (there's archaeological evidence for this since Neanderthals).

### 8.4 Reputation, gossip and norms
- **Reputation** is what others believe about someone (generous, stingy, brave, liar, skilled healer), spread by **gossip** through the social network, with decay and distortion.
- **Norms** come from the culture (§9): sharing rules, marriage rules, taboos, etiquette, property rules, rules about violence. Agents notice violations (§6.2), feel indignation, gossip, and apply **sanctions** scaled by severity and culture: ridicule, avoidance, withheld cooperation, demands for compensation, ostracism, exile, and (in some cultures and eras) violence or formal punishment. In egalitarian foragers, **leveling mechanisms** (mockery of boasting, demand sharing) keep status flat.

### 8.5 Status, leadership and decisions
- Status comes from culturally valued sources: skill, generosity, age, kinship position, ritual knowledge, wealth (later eras), office (state eras).
- Group decisions (where to camp, when to move, whether to accept a stranger, war or peace) by culture-appropriate procedure: consensus discussion among adults in bands, elders' councils, big-man persuasion, chiefly decree, institutional law (§13). Agents argue for options according to their interests, values and personalities.

### 8.6 Conflict
Interpersonal conflict escalates realistically (disagreement → argument → threat → fight, with most de-escalating through avoidance, mediation, compensation or leaving the group). Group conflict: feuds, raids, territorial disputes, alliances by marriage and exchange, and in later eras, organized warfare (abstracted per §1.4). Contested resources (water in drought, game in lean years, farmland) raise conflict risk, as in history.

### 8.7 Strangers
How a group treats an unknown person depends on culture, era, recent history and personality: wariness, ritualized greeting, testing, gift exchange, hospitality norms, hostility in contested territory. Strangers gain trust slowly through behavior. **This is how the player meets humans** (§16).

## 9. Culture

### 9.1 Generated cultures
Each population in the deep-time layer carries a **culture**: a data structure generated from realistic cross-cultural patterns, constrained by era profile (§15.3), subsistence, environment and history, and then evolved over time. It includes:
- **Subsistence and economy:** foraging strategy (mobile vs semi-sedentary, hunting/gathering/fishing mix, seasonal round), later farming and herding mixes, storage, exchange forms.
- **Social organization:** group sizes, residence rules (patrilocal, matrilocal, bilocal, neolocal), descent (patrilineal, matrilineal, bilateral), marriage system (monogamy norms, permitted polygyny, bride-price/bride-service/dowry, arranged vs chosen), inheritance, age grades.
- **Division of labor** and roles by age and sex (varying across cultures; §1.6) and specialists in later eras.
- **Values** along realistic dimensions (e.g., egalitarian ↔ hierarchical, kin-centered ↔ wider cooperation, honor-based conflict ↔ conciliation, tightness of norm enforcement).
- **Belief system:** a generated cosmology appropriate to era and lifeway (animistic and ancestral beliefs and shamanic practice for foragers; ancestor cults, fertility and seasonal rites for early farmers; priesthoods, temples and pantheons in state societies), explanations for illness, death, weather and luck, taboos, an afterlife idea, sacred places in the actual landscape (a striking peak, a spring, a cave).
- **Ritual and expressive culture:** rites of passage, mourning, seasonal festivals, music and dance (simple procedural music), body ornament, art style (procedural motifs used on tools, clothing, cave walls, pottery), storytelling traditions.
- **Material culture style:** preferred tool forms, clothing styles, house forms, decoration, from the same process and construction systems, so a group's things look like *theirs*.
- **Food customs** (staples, preparation, taboos), **etiquette** (greetings, hospitality, gift norms), **child-rearing** style.

### 9.2 Transmission and evolution
- Children absorb culture from caregivers and peers; adults keep updating through conformity and prestige biases (§5.1).
- Cultures **drift** (random changes in style, ritual details, words), **adapt** (practices that work in an environment spread; useless ones fade), **diffuse** across contact (trade partners and neighbors borrow tools, words and ideas), and **split** when groups separate, diverging into daughter cultures.
- Individuals deviate: nonconformists exist, and innovation often starts with them (§11.4).

## 10. Language and communication

### 10.1 Languages
- Procedurally generated languages with a phonology (sound inventory, syllable structure), a lexicon generated from meanings, simple grammar templates (word order, a few affixes), and naming conventions (personal names, place names that describe the landscape, kin terms).
- **Language families** arise in deep time: when populations split, their languages drift apart through regular sound changes and vocabulary replacement, so related languages share recognizable words. Contact produces loanwords; long contact produces bilingualism.
- Species profiles gate language: Australopithecus have calls and gestures only; *H. erectus* a simple proto-language (gesture-rich); Neanderthals and *H. sapiens* full language.

### 10.2 Speech acts (the deterministic core)
All communication between Persons (including the player) is **speech acts** with structured content: greet, introduce, ask (about place, person, thing, how-to), inform, warn, request, offer, accept, refuse, thank, apologize, praise, insult, joke, threaten, command (where status allows), teach (§11.2), tell story, gossip, propose (pair bond, alliance, trade, plan), and argue for/against a group option. Gestures (point, show, offer, beckon, shoo, display threat or submission) work without shared language.

Agents choose speech acts through their mind (§6) and **only about things they know or believe**. The act's effect on beliefs, relationships and knowledge is computed deterministically.

### 10.3 Rendering
- Speech is shown as subtitles (and optional generated voice-like vocalizations using the language's phonology). Text is rendered from the speech act via grammar templates into the speaker's language, and **translated for the player according to the player character's knowledge of that language**: unknown words stay in the foreign language, partially learned ones show with uncertain glosses.
- The player learns languages by exposure and interaction (faster when people teach; slower when only overheard), as v2's knowledge system tracks it.

### 10.4 Optional conversation backend (pluggable, off by default)
For richer conversations near the player, provide a `ConversationBackend` trait:
- **None (default):** template rendering only; the player talks with a speech-act wheel plus gestures.
- **Local model / remote API (user-configured):** used for (a) phrasing agents' speech acts as natural lines, and (b) letting the player type free text that is **parsed into a speech act** (with a confirmation fallback when ambiguous).
- **Guardrails (mandatory):** the backend receives only the speaker's knowledge, beliefs, personality, emotional state, relationship to the listener, culture and era vocabulary; it can never introduce facts the agent doesn't know; outputs pass an **anachronism and knowledge filter** (no concepts, words or technology outside the agent's knowledge state); the simulation state changes only through the structured speech act, never through free text. Agents' decisions never depend on the backend, so the world stays deterministic. The game is fully playable without it.

## 11. Knowledge, learning and innovation (humans and the knowledge graph)

Agents use the v2 knowledge graph and process engine (v2 §11–12) like the player does.

### 11.1 Knowing
Each Person's `Knowledge` holds known nodes, skill levels, places, people and languages. Agents **never use technology they don't know**, and they can't plan with materials or places they don't know about. The **anachronism guard** (a runtime assertion and a test) checks that no agent ever performs a process outside its knowledge state.

### 11.2 Learning from others
Realistic social learning, from cheapest to richest:
- **Observation and imitation:** watching a skilled person accumulates insight (the existing v2 hominin → player mechanism, now general).
- **Teaching:** a knower demonstrates and corrects; much faster than discovery; species-gated (minimal in Australopithecus, real in *H. sapiens*).
- **Apprenticeship:** long-term pairing of learner and expert for complex skills (knapping fine blades, pottery, metallurgy), across months or years of calendar time.
- **Storytelling** at the evening fire spreads knowledge of places, dangers, history and norms through the group, with distortion over retellings.
- **Records** (later eras): marks, tallies and eventually writing preserve knowledge beyond memory (planned eras).
Teaching the player is one of the most valuable things humans offer, and something the player can also do for them.

### 11.3 Discovery by agents
Agents can discover new knowledge using the same routes as the player (v2 §12.3: experimentation, observation of nature, inference), at rates set by personality (curiosity, novelty seeking), need pressure (scarcity drives experimentation), time available (surplus frees time), materials at hand, and luck.

### 11.4 Cumulative culture (the "collective brain")
The rate of innovation and the risk of losing knowledge depend on **population size and how connected people are**: large, well-connected populations accumulate and keep complex skills; small, isolated groups can lose skills that too few people know (as happened historically when small populations were cut off). Model this explicitly: each knowledge node has a minimum number of skilled practitioners and a transmission fidelity; if too few people know it and teach it, the culture loses it. Diffusion between groups follows contact (trade, marriage exchange, migration). This is the engine that makes humanity's development emerge rather than follow a script.

## 12. Work, economy and daily life

- **Daily and seasonal routines** by era, culture, role, age and season (data), so a day in a forager camp looks different from a day in a Neolithic village or a Bronze Age town, and people in the same place still differ.
- **Work** uses the real systems: foragers gather and hunt with v2 mechanics; farmers sow, weed, harvest and store on the right seasons; builders build with the v2 structural system; craftspeople make things through the process engine.
- **Economy by era:** generalized sharing and reciprocity → household production and storage → specialization and exchange (barter, gift networks, markets) → commodity money and coinage (Iron Age/Classical) → planned systems later. **Property norms** come from culture: few personal possessions and shared territory for mobile foragers; household and lineage property in farming societies; land ownership, inheritance and debt in states.
- **Settlements** are built by agents through the v2 construction system, using culture-specific blueprint vocabularies (§9.1) assembled from household needs: hearths, shelters, storage, workshops, pens, paths, communal and ritual spaces. They grow, shrink and get abandoned for real reasons (resources, conflict, disease, soil exhaustion, climate).

## 13. Society scale and politics

Political organization emerges from population, density, surplus, inequality and conflict, following the broad cross-cultural pattern (not a fixed ladder; regression happens):
- **Bands** (mobile foragers; egalitarian; leadership by persuasion and skill).
- **Tribes** (linked villages or bands; clans and lineages; councils; big men in some cultures).
- **Chiefdoms** (hereditary ranking, redistribution, a chief's center).
- **Early states** (cities, rulers, administration, taxation or tribute, specialists, law, standing forces).
Transitions are triggered by thresholds in the simulation (e.g., settled population, stored surplus, external threat) with probabilities shaped by culture. Institutions (offices, councils, laws, temples, markets) are data structures with rules agents follow and can contest.

## 14. Health and demography

### 14.1 Illness
Extend v2 §9.6 to populations: infectious diseases spread through contact networks, with transmission rates depending on density, sanitation, water and animal contact. **Crowd diseases** appear only once settlements are dense enough and animals are domesticated (Neolithic onward), as historically. Epidemics happen and can reshape societies. Immunity and resistance partly genetic (§4.1) and partly acquired. Medical knowledge (the v2 medicinal plants, then later practices) affects outcomes modestly.

### 14.2 Nutrition
Agent diets come from what they actually gather, hunt, grow and store, through the v2 nutrition model, so famine years, shortages and diet shifts (e.g., early farmers' poorer dental and stature outcomes) emerge.

### 14.3 Life tables (validation targets)
Data holds per-era target demographics, and long headless runs must land near them (record sources and mark uncertain values):
- **Foragers:** high infant and child mortality; life expectancy at birth around the low-to-mid 30s, but people who survive childhood often live into their 60s–70s; total fertility roughly 4–6 with birth intervals around 3–4 years; long-run growth near zero; residential groups ~25–50 within wider networks of hundreds.
- **Early farmers:** higher fertility and shorter birth intervals, larger settlements, slow population growth, more infectious disease.
- **Later eras:** per-era targets authored with their era.

## 15. History simulation and eras

### 15.1 Deep-time layer (world creation)
A fast, abstract simulation over the planet's real geography, run at world creation behind a progress bar (target: a few minutes at most for any era start on the reference machine; cache the result in the world):
- Starts from one or more **cradle regions** (tropical savanna-woodland, by default the one with the best conditions), with hominin species appearing in the order and places the era profile specifies.
- Simulates **populations on the ecological cell grid** (v2 §7.4) as demographic units with gene pools (§4.5), cultures (§9), languages (§10.1) and knowledge distributions (§11.4): growth limited by carrying capacity of the actual biomes and the technology known, dispersal along coasts, rivers and open land, crossings of straits within reach of known boats, land bridges when the era's sea level exposes them, retreat from ice and deserts.
- Produces **cultural and linguistic geography** (splits, contacts, borrowings), **knowledge geography** (where innovations arose and how far they spread), **domestication** in regions with suitable wild species (v2 §7.7 ancestors and Appendix A crop ancestors), settlement sites and a **chronicle** of major events.

### 15.2 Recent-history layer (around the spawn)
For the region around the player's chosen spawn (e.g., the populations within a few days' walk), run the **household tier** (§17) for the last few generations (default 3–4 generations, ~100 years), producing **real individuals with genealogies, life histories, relationships, reputations, skills, possessions and memories**, plus their settlements built through real construction. When the player arrives, the people they meet already have lives.

### 15.3 Era profiles
Each era profile (data) defines species present and their regions, knowledge baseline ranges by region, society scale, subsistence, typical group and settlement sizes, institutions available, routines, material culture vocabulary, belief-system templates, life tables and climate offsets (v2 §17.1). The player selects an era at world creation (v2 §16.1); the deep-time sim runs until the world reaches that era's state.

Eras and how they should feel:
| Era | Who you meet | How it should feel |
|---|---|---|
| **Wild Earth** (current default) | Sporadic *Australopithecus* groups | Wild animals dominate; the hominins are rare, wary and curious (v2 §8) |
| **Lower Paleolithic** | *Homo erectus* bands | Small groups ranging widely; gesture and simple proto-language; hand axes; fire kept and carried |
| **Middle Paleolithic** | Neanderthals in cold northern realms, early *H. sapiens* in warmer ones, possible contact zones | Small, close-knit bands; fire-making; hafted spears; care for the injured; burials; limited symbolic culture |
| **Upper Paleolithic** | *H. sapiens* foragers | Full language; bands within regional tribes linked by marriage and exchange; seasonal aggregation camps; art, music, ornament, stories; long-distance trade in shells and fine stone |
| **Neolithic** | Villages of farmers and herders (foragers persist at the margins) | Fields, pens, granaries, permanent houses, lineages, ancestor rites, feasts, emerging inequality, raids over land |
| **Bronze Age** | Chiefdoms and early states, towns and cities | Specialists, markets and long-distance trade for copper and tin, temples and priests, rulers, record marks, organized conflict |
| **Iron Age / Classical** | Kingdoms and city-states | Iron tools and farming expansion, roads, coinage, law codes, armies, monumental building |
| **Medieval → Modern** (planned) | — | Authored as planned profiles; systems listed in v2 §12.2 and `docs/design/future-systems.md` |

**Implementation order:** Wild Earth and the three Paleolithic eras first (they only need tech from v2 Eras 0–2, which exist), then Neolithic, Bronze Age and Iron Age as their technology milestones land (§20).

### 15.4 Observer (Chronicle) mode
A spectator mode, available from the main menu for any world and in-game (where the world settings allow), for **watching humanity develop**:
- Time controls from real-time to fast-forward. At high speeds, simulation shifts down to the household and demographic tiers (§17) and the view switches to the map/globe.
- Map overlays: population density, cultures and languages, knowledge frontiers (where each technology is known), settlements, trade and marriage networks, conflicts, gene-pool appearance averages, disease outbreaks.
- A **Chronicle** log of notable events (a group crossing a strait, an innovation, a new settlement, a famine, a war, a new culture splitting off), each clickable to jump there.
- Follow any individual or family, and read their life history.
- **Step in:** start playing at any point, as a newcomer arriving in a chosen place, or (setting) as a member of a chosen family.

### 15.5 Era authenticity checklist
For each era, `docs/review/era-<name>.md` captures screenshots and agent logs of a sample week and checks: group sizes, daily routines, work and division of labor, tools only from known nodes, food and diet, settlements and architecture, dress, ritual and belief behaviors, treatment of strangers, conflict levels, demography against life tables, and the "nothing looks scripted" variety test.

## 17. Simulation tiers, persistence and performance

### 17.1 Tiers
| Tier | Who | Fidelity | Update rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Full** | People within simulation distance of the player (target up to ~150–300 humans) | Complete Person components; real movement, processes, construction, conversation | Reflexes every tick; utility selection staggered every few game seconds; planning on demand |
| **Household** | People in the recent-history region and anywhere the player has social ties (target tens of thousands of individuals) | Individual `Person` records (genome, kin, key traits, relationships, compressed memories, skills, possessions) with coarse daily outcomes per household (food got, work done, events) instead of moment-to-moment actions | Once per game day (or per season at fast-forward) |
| **Demographic** | Everyone else on the planet | Populations per cell with gene pools, culture, language, knowledge distribution | Seasonal or yearly steps |

- **Promotion and demotion conserve state.** A household promoted to Full gets its members back with their histories; a Full person demoted keeps everything important. A demographic population that comes into the household region is **instantiated** into individuals with consistent genealogies synthesized back a few generations from the gene pool, culture and settlement history, and those people then persist.
- **Persistent persons:** anyone who has met the player, is kin to the player, or is notable in the chronicle always keeps an individual record.
- Storage stays bounded: compressed LifeHistory (§6.3), compact genomes (§4.1), and pruning of long-dead individuals to genealogy stubs (id, parents, dates, name).

### 17.2 Budgets (reference machine from v1/v2)
- Full-tier humans: within ~3 ms per tick together with v2's animal budget, multithreaded; never blocking the main thread.
- Household tier: one game day for 10,000 people in under ~50 ms of background work.
- Deep-time history: a world creation for a Bronze Age start in a few minutes at most, with a progress bar; Paleolithic starts much faster.
- Observer fast-forward: at least ~10 game years per real second at the demographic tier on the reference machine.

## 2. Death: what happens next

### 2.1 The moment of death
- Your character's death is a real event in the world. The body stays where you fell. Your people react as their culture dictates: mourning, a burial or other rites, and inheritance of your possessions and roles (V2.1 §7.4).
- A **death screen** shows the story of the life you lived: age, where you were born, family, children, people who mourn you, places reached, things you learned, things you taught.
- Then you choose one of the options below. The choice screen can be reopened from spectating at any time.

### 2.2 The four choices

**1. Inhabit another person.** Continue as any eligible living human (§2.3), adult or child.
- Pick from a list with filters: *my family*, *my group*, *near where I died*, *anywhere* (on the globe). Each entry shows only what your dead character could plausibly have known about them (name if known, relationship, rough age, group). If they're strangers, the entry shows a short description of their household instead. As with births, **you don't see what they look like before choosing.**
- You take over their body and life: their knowledge and skills, relationships, obligations, possessions, reputation, health and injuries. Their own mind steps aside; the rest of the world doesn't know anything changed. People may still notice that someone "isn't acting like themselves", through the ordinary social systems.
- A **"Who you are" briefing** opens: name, age, family tree, the people who matter to them and how they feel about each, obligations and debts, recent memories (from their episodic memory), and their journal rebuilt from their knowledge.
- If you inhabit someone nearby, you may find yourself at your previous character's funeral. That's intended.
- **Inhabiting a child:**
  - *Single-player:* the remaining childhood follows Addendum A's flow from the child's current age (short playable moments, years passing quickly), and you're safe throughout.
  - *Multiplayer:* world time is shared, so childhood passes at the world's pace. Show the real-time estimate before confirming ("about N hours of play per year of age at this server's calendar"). The child-safety rule applies in full.

**2. Be born again.** Start a new life as a baby.
- *Single-player:* run Addendum A's birth flow (choose a place on the globe, choose from 2–4 births from real households, daughter / son / chance).
- *Multiplayer:* use **Remembered Childhood** (§3.3), since the world can't fast-forward for one player.

**3. Restart the world.**
- *Single-player:* either **Replay this world from the beginning** (same seed and settings, fresh history) or **Start a new world** (Create World with the current settings pre-filled). The old save is always kept and archived; it's deleted only if you explicitly choose to.
- *Multiplayer:* owner and admins only. Other players see "Only the host can restart this world" and can choose to leave the server instead.

**4. Spectate.**
- *Single-player:* opens **Observer mode** (V2.1 §15.4) with its time controls, overlays and chronicle.
- *Multiplayer:* a free camera that can follow any person or animal. No time controls (admins excepted). Spectators are invisible and inaudible to the living, and nothing in the simulation perceives them. Spectators get a **spectator chat** among themselves; whether spectators can speak to living players is a server setting, **off by default** (no ghost-scouting).
- From spectating, you can open the choice screen and inhabit someone or be born again whenever you like.

### 2.3 Who can be inhabited
- **Never:** another player's current character.
- **Only with that player's consent** (a prompt they can accept or decline): anyone in another player's immediate family (partner, children, parents, siblings).
- **Not right now:** someone who is currently in a fight, being chased, badly injured and dying, or giving birth. This prevents last-second escapes and keeps those moments respectful.
- **Scope** (world/server setting):

| Setting | Who's eligible | Default |
|---|---|---|
| *Anyone* | Any eligible living human on the planet | **Single-player default** |
| *Kin, group and region* | Your family, your group, or anyone in the region where you died | **Multiplayer default** |
| *Kin only* | Living relatives only | — |
| *None* | Inhabiting disabled (permadeath-style worlds); be born again, restart and spectate remain | — |

### 2.4 What you keep (world setting "Knowledge after death")

| Setting | Effect | Default for |
|---|---|---|
| **Their knowledge only** | You know exactly what the new person knows. Your old journal stays readable as *notes from a past life* but unlocks nothing. | **Authentic** |
| **Head start** | Things your previous characters discovered are rediscovered much faster (insight thresholds reduced). | — |
| **Keep everything** | Your discovered knowledge carries over at beginner skill. | **Hardy** |

Skills are always the new person's own. Relationships, reputation and possessions are always the new person's own; your old character's are inherited by whoever their culture says.

### 2.5 Permadeath
"Permadeath" is now simply a preset: Inhabit scope = *None* and "Be born again" disabled. On death you can spectate, restart, or end the world with the life story saved.

## 3. Being born in multiplayer

### 3.1 The problem
In single-player, childhood moves the whole world forward years at a time (Addendum A). In a shared world that can only happen if **everyone online is a child at the same time**. So multiplayer has two ways to be born, plus family links between players.

### 3.2 Shared start (friends starting a world together)
- At world creation, or when the host opens a **new** world, the host can choose **Start together**. Every player who joins the start picks a family arrangement in a short lobby:
  - **Twins**: born together into one household. Fraternal by default. *Identical* twins (same genome, same sex) are allowed as an explicit choice; this is a liberty, since real identical twins are rare.
  - **Siblings**: born 1–4 years apart into one household, with the spacing realistic for the era.
  - **Cousins**: children of siblings, in the same group.
  - **Neighbors**: different households in the same band or village.
  - **Apart**: each player chooses their own place on the globe.
- Each player still picks daughter / son / chance. Looks still come only from the parents' genes and are never shown in advance.
- The births are drawn from **real households** that fit the arrangement. If none fit exactly, pick the closest fit and say so in the lobby. History generation already allows for this (§3.5).
- **Shared childhood:** the server runs Addendum A's childhood flow for the whole group. The short playable moments are played **together** (kids exploring, learning from the same elders, getting into the same trouble), and the years-passing jumps happen for the whole world. The phase ends when the youngest player comes of age, or earlier if the host ends it (remaining children then grow up at the world's pace).
- A player who misses a session during shared childhood stays a child of their family run by the family's normal care (a child agent following routines). Joining back resumes play at the current age.
- A player who joins **during** shared childhood can be born into the group at the next jump (for example, as a younger sibling) and continues on the remaining schedule.

### 3.3 Remembered Childhood (joining a world that's already running)
When a player joins a world whose time can't be fast-forwarded for them (or chooses "Be born again" in multiplayer), their childhood is played **as memories**:
1. **Choose a place** on the globe, within the host's allowed regions.
2. **Choose from 2–4 births** from real households there. These births happened about 13–16 years ago (the coming-of-age age comes from the culture's data). Each option is a real young person in the world's records who:
   - has **never been met** by any player (no player and no player's character has a relationship with them or a memory of them), and
   - isn't in another player's immediate family, unless that player consents (§3.4).
   Daughter / son / chance filters the options. Looks aren't shown.
3. **Play their childhood as memories.** A sequence of short playable vignettes (default about 45–90 minutes in total, configurable; skippable at any point) reconstructed from that person's recorded life history in the household tier (V2.1 §15.2, §17): their family, home, the seasons they lived through, the people who raised them. Each vignette runs in a **private, isolated instance** of that moment of the past, seeded from the record.
4. **What you do in memories matters, within limits.** It shapes what this young person learned (knowledge and skills), how warm their relationships are, and small details of their life history. It **can't contradict fixed facts**: who lived and died, where the family lived, major events, and anything any player has witnessed. You can't die or leave the region in a memory. Results are committed to the person's record when the memories end.
5. **Wake in the present** as that young person, in their home, among people who've known you all your life. To everyone else, including other players, you've always been there.
- "Skip memories" uses the young person's default recorded upbringing.
- Determinism: memory instances are seeded and isolated; only the final committed record touches the shared world.

### 3.4 Being born into a friend's family later on
For a player joining an existing world who wants to be with a friend who's already playing:
- **Sibling or cousin:** choose one of the friend's **existing** siblings or cousins of a suitable age (people the friend's character already knows). This requires the friend's consent, and uses Remembered Childhood for that person. The memories are reconstructed to stay consistent with the friend's character's shared history with them.
- **Same group:** choose a never-met young person in the friend's group (no consent needed).
- **Child of a player:** a player with a partner (V2.1 pair bond; mutual consent as always) can **invite** someone to be born as their child. The joining player waits through the pregnancy (shown as time remaining at the world's pace) while spectating or doing anything else on the server, then is born as a newborn whose childhood passes at the world's pace (with the real-time warning from §2.2). Show this clearly; it's a long commitment, chosen deliberately.

### 3.5 Server settings for births
In `server.toml` and the host UI:
- **Birth regions:** anywhere / host-chosen regions / near existing players.
- **Shared start:** on / off.
- **Remembered Childhood length** and whether it's skippable.
- **Family links between players:** allowed / same group only / off.
- **Expected player count**, used by history generation so there are enough suitable households for births (especially in **Wild Earth**, where human families are few and far apart: generate a few more wandering families when more players are expected, still far apart unless players choose to be born together).
- **Inhabit scope** and **Knowledge after death** (§2.3–2.4).

## 4. Other multiplayer details

- **Logging off:**
  - *Adults:* default as in Amendment R (the character stays in the world asleep or resting in a safe place). New option: **"Live on quietly while I'm away"**, where the character keeps doing their normal routines under the game's own mind. While AI-run, the character makes no major life decisions (no pair bonds, no leaving the group, no risky hunts, no fights it can avoid).
  - *Child player characters:* always cared for by their family as a normal child while the player is away.
- **Child safety in multiplayer:** a player whose character is a child can never be harmed by anyone: players, agents or animals. Adult players can't target child characters in any PvP setting. Family agents keep player children safe exactly as in Addendum A.
- **Families between players are real families:** genetics (siblings resemble each other through their shared parents), kinship, inheritance and obligations work the same as for agents. Agents treat player siblings as siblings.
- **Voice as a child:** proximity voice works as usual. Children pick up languages faster (V2.1 §7.3), including the player character's understanding of the local language.

## 6. Tests to add
- Inhabiting transfers exactly the target's knowledge, skills, relationships, obligations and possessions; the old character's possessions follow their culture's inheritance.
- Eligibility rules: players' characters, unconsented kin and "not right now" people are never offered.
- Restart never deletes a save without explicit confirmation.
- Spectators are never perceived by any agent or animal, and can't talk to the living when the setting is off.
- Remembered Childhood never contradicts fixed facts or anything a player has witnessed, never offers a person any player has met, and is deterministic for the same seed and choices.
- Shared start produces the requested family arrangement (or the closest fit, reported), and shared childhood jumps advance the whole world consistently for every connected client.
- Child player characters can't be harmed by any source in any PvP setting.
